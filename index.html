    Method

    The tool follows one idea: a tariff works best on products the target country depends on selling to you, that you do not depend on buying from it, and that the rest of the world can supply instead.

    Three ratios per product

    Export dependenceTarget's exports to you ÷ target's exports to the world. High means the target has few other buyers. Import dependenceYour imports from the target ÷ your imports from the world. Low means the tariff barely touches your own supply. Above the cap (50% by default) the product is dropped: the tariff would hit you harder than the target. SubstitutionExports of the product by third countries (the world minus you and the target) ÷ what you buy from the target. Below 1× the product is dropped: nobody else could fill the gap.

    Ranking

    Exposed valueTrade value × export dependence × (1 − import dependence). The part of the flow the target could not easily sell elsewhere, discounted by the cost to you. Dependence onlyExport dependence × (1 − import dependence). Surfaces small, highly exposed niches, often the politically sensitive ones.

    Exposed value is a score in dollars, not a forecast of losses. It does not know the tariff rate, how prices would react or how long redirecting sales would take. Use it to compare products and countries, not to predict what anyone would lose.

    Ratios for chapters and headings are computed from summed trade flows, not averaged across products, so large products weigh more.

    Three fixed rules

    • You never tariff a product if too much of your imports of it come from the target. The default cap is 50%; "Max. reliance on the target" lets you try a stricter 30% or a bolder 70%, but the cap cannot be switched off.
    • The target must rely on you more than you rely on it for that product (export dependence above import dependence). Otherwise the tariff hurts you more than them.
    • Third countries must be able to replace the target (substitution of at least 1×).

    The second rule is the idea of asymmetric interdependence: trade gives power to the side that needs the other less. Albert Hirschman set it out in National Power and the Structure of Foreign Trade (1945), and Robert Keohane and Joseph Nye developed it in Power and Interdependence (1977).

    Leverage index

    Before asking where to strike, a country should ask whether to strike at all. The index adds up the exposed value of every qualifying product in both directions and compares them: exposed value of B to A ÷ (exposed value of B to A + exposed value of A to B), from 0 to 100. Above 50, the country imposing tariffs has the stronger hand; below 50, the target could retaliate harder.

    The 50% cap came from testing. Without it, the model told South Korea to tariff Chinese memory chips, a product Korea buys almost entirely from China, much of it made in Korean-owned plants there. A discount on dependence was not enough; above a threshold the product has to go.

    Data

    CEPII BACI, built from UN Comtrade. BACI reconciles what exporters and importers report into one value per flow, at the 6-digit Harmonized System level (about 5,600 products). Values are yearly averages in US dollars.

    What the model leaves out

    • Political and strategic judgement. Energy and medicines score high because they are large flows, yet governments rarely tariff them. Use the "Leave out" filters to see the difference.
    • Supply chains. Tariffs on components raise costs for your own manufacturers, even when other suppliers exist.
    • Time. Switching suppliers takes contracts, logistics and months; the substitution ratio measures capacity, not speed.
    • Reaction. It is a snapshot, not a model of escalation, WTO rules or exchange rates.
    • Sanctions are simplified: for Western countries, Russia, Belarus, Iran and North Korea are not counted as alternative suppliers.
    • EU member states share one trade policy, so they can be targeted but only the EU as a whole can retaliate.
